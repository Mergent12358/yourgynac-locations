When Your Period Comes After I-Pill, and When to Take a Test

The wait for the next period can feel longer than the night you took I-Pill. A lot of women expect the cycle to stay exactly on its usual date, then get anxious when bleeding comes early, comes late, or shows up only as spotting.

I-Pill is a levonorgestrel 1.5 mg emergency contraceptive. Because it can affect ovulation and the cycle you are in, your period may not behave as it usually does. It might arrive a few days early. It might be delayed. The flow can be lighter or a little different from your normal period. Spotting in the days after the tablet is also something women commonly notice. None of this, on its own, tells you whether the pill worked. The more useful question is whether a proper period arrives, and when.

If your period is more than a week late, take a pregnancy test. If the test is negative and your period still does not come, test again or speak with a gynaecologist rather than guessing. This short note on the period after I-Pill is a useful companion if you are trying to tell spotting apart from a period, or decide when a test actually makes sense.

Until that period arrives, do not treat I-Pill as cover for the rest of the month. It does not protect you against pregnancy if you have unprotected sex again, and it does not protect against sexually transmitted infections. Use a condom or another method if you need contraception going forward. I-Pill is also not an abortion pill. It is meant to reduce the chance of pregnancy after unprotected sex, not to end a pregnancy that has already begun.

If bleeding is unusually heavy, or you have strong pain, dizziness, or a test result you cannot make sense of, get medical advice. Bring the date you took the tablet and the date your last period started. Those details save time.
